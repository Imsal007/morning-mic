How to stop saying um and other filler words

By the Founder Rehearsal team · Updated 25 November 2026 · 6 min read

The short answer. You stop saying um by replacing it with a short silent pause. Record yourself, count your fillers, learn your first sentence by heart, and repeat the same answer on several days. Aim for fewer fillers, not zero.

If you have ever played back a pitch and winced at the "um, so, basically", you are not alone. Fillers are normal in everyday talk. They become a problem when they are so frequent that listeners notice them more than your point. This guide shows you how to reduce them without sounding stiff or scripted.

Why do people say um?

When you speak without a script, you are planning the next idea while still saying the last one. When the planning falls behind, your mouth keeps going and produces a sound that holds your place: um, uh, er. Phrases such as like, you know, kind of, sort of and basically often do the same job.

For founders, three moments tend to produce the most fillers:

  • The opening of an answer. You start speaking before you know your first sentence.
  • The switch between ideas. You finish the problem and have not decided how to get to the product.
  • A question you did not expect. You think out loud while trying to sound certain.

Notice what these have in common: each is a planning problem. That is why "just stop saying um" rarely works on its own. You need to know where you are going and be comfortable being quiet while you get there.

Do filler words really matter?

One small study suggests they can. In a Cal Poly experiment with 145 participants, people listened to recorded interview answers with either no fillers or many. The more fillers a speaker used, the lower listeners rated their professional and personal credibility. It was a student project using short recordings, so treat it as a useful signal and not a law.

The practical lesson is about frequency. A stray "um" in a good answer will rarely lose you a customer. An answer where every sentence starts with "so, um, basically" makes a listener wonder whether you know your own business.

How to stop saying um: seven steps

1. Count before you change anything

Set a timer for 60 seconds and answer this: "What does your company do, and who is it for?" Record it on your phone. Play it back and make a mark for every filler. Write down the total and the date. This number is your baseline.

2. Find your personal fillers

Most people have two or three favourites. List yours. It is much easier to catch "basically" than to watch for every possible filler at once.

3. Find where they happen

Play the recording again and note what came just before each filler. Was it the start of a sentence? A move to a new idea? A number you were not sure of? The pattern tells you what to practise.

4. Swap the sound for a pause

Where you would say "um", close your mouth and breathe in through your nose. A pause of about a second feels long to you, but it usually sounds deliberate to a listener. Closing your lips matters, because it is hard to say "um" with your mouth shut.

5. Memorise first sentences, not scripts

Do not learn your whole pitch word for word: if you lose one line, you can lose the thread. Learn only the first sentence of your most common answers, such as what you do, why you started, who pays you and what you are raising. A confident first sentence gives you time to plan the second. Our guide to explaining your startup in 60 seconds has a template.

6. Use shorter sentences

Long sentences joined with "and" and "so" create more places to get lost. Say one idea, stop, then say the next. For example, replace "So we're basically a tool that, um, helps clinics, and also, like, reminds patients" with "We help clinics cut missed appointments. We do it with automatic reminders."

7. Repeat the same answer on different days

Answer the same question several times across a week. Because the content stays the same, you can hear your delivery change. Compare day one with day seven. This is far more useful than a general feeling that you are improving.

A 14-day plan to reduce filler words

DaysFocusWhat to do (about 10 minutes)
1 to 2AwarenessRecord your 60-second answer. Count fillers. List your top three.
3 to 5The pauseRead a short paragraph aloud and pause fully at every full stop. Then record your answer with closed-mouth pauses.
6 to 8OpenersWrite and learn a first sentence for five common questions. Practise starting each one cold.
9 to 11TransitionsLearn one bridge phrase between sections, for example "Here is what we built." Practise moving from problem to product.
12 to 14PressureDraw a random hard question, wait two seconds, then answer. Count again and compare with day one.

The plan does not promise a particular result. People start with different habits. What it gives you is a number you can compare fairly, because you measure the same answer the same way.

What can you say instead of um?

The best replacement is nothing. When you need a few seconds for a hard question, use a full, honest sentence:

  • "Let me think about that for a moment."
  • "There are two parts to that. First, ..."
  • "The short answer is yes. Here is why."

Use these sparingly. If you start every answer with "That's a great question", it becomes a new filler.

Mistakes that keep the ums coming

  • Policing yourself mid-pitch. Count on the recording, not while you talk. In the real meeting, think about the listener.
  • Speeding up to avoid silence. Rushing leaves less time to plan. See how to speak slower and more clearly.
  • Practising only in your head. Fillers appear when you speak, so you have to practise aloud.
  • Changing the question every time. You cannot compare two different answers.

Try this now: the five-minute drill

  1. Record 60 seconds on "What does your company do?" Count the fillers.
  2. Write one opening sentence and say it aloud three times.
  3. Record the answer again. Close your mouth wherever you want to say um.
  4. Count again and write both numbers down with today's date.

Questions people ask

Is it normal to say um a lot?

Yes. Almost everyone uses fillers in unscripted speech, and they often increase when you are nervous or answering something new. It is worth working on when fillers are frequent enough to distract from your point.

What can I replace um with?

Replace it with a short silent pause. When you need more thinking time, say a complete sentence such as "Let me think about that for a moment."

How do I stop umming and ahhing in presentations?

Learn your first sentence and the line that links each section, practise aloud, and pause with your mouth closed. Count fillers on recordings, not during the presentation.

Why do I say like and basically so much?

They usually fill the same gap as um while you decide what comes next. Pick one to work on at a time, find where it appears on a recording, and practise a pause there.

How long does it take to stop saying um?

It varies from person to person, so no fixed timeline is reliable. Record the same answer every few days and compare your count.

Is it bad to say um in a job interview or investor meeting?

A few are unlikely to matter. Heavy use can make you sound less sure, and one small study linked many fillers with lower credibility ratings.
