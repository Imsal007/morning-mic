How to answer hard investor questions without waffling

By the Founder Rehearsal team · Updated 25 November 2026 · 5 min read

The short answer. Pause, answer the question in your first sentence, give one reason or fact, then stop. If you don't know, say so, explain what you do know and say how you will find out.

A rehearsed pitch can go well and still unravel in the questions. The usual problem is not a lack of knowledge. It is a long answer that circles the point. This guide gives you a structure to practise, so your answers stay short when the pressure rises.

The structure: Answer, Reason, Stop

  1. Pause. One or two seconds. Check you understood the question.
  2. Answer it in the first sentence.
  3. Reason: give one fact or example that supports the answer.
  4. Stop. Let the investor decide whether to go further.

For a challenge or objection, add one step at the front: acknowledge what is fair about it. "Yes, that's a real risk. Here is how we're testing it." Acknowledging is not the same as giving in.

If a question is unclear, ask: "Do you mean the cost to acquire a customer, or the cost to serve one?" A short clarifying question is better than answering the wrong one.

Know the ideas behind the questions

In How to Pitch Your Company, Michael Seibel of Y Combinator walks through core questions, including what you do, the size of the market, your progress, what you know that others don't, how you make money, who is on the team and what you are asking for. On progress, he explains that he wants to understand how fast you produce work. On your insight, he says it usually comes from many conversations with customers, close analysis of existing products and often personal experience. Prepare those before the clever questions.

12 hard questions and how to approach them

The sample answers use a made-up dental reminder company. Square brackets mark facts you must replace with your own. Never use a number you can't support.

"What do you do?"Clarity"We fill empty appointment slots for dental practices."
"Why now?"Timing"[The specific change] means practices can do this today when they couldn't before."
"Who else does this?"Honesty and market knowledge"[Names]. Practices also use phone calls. We differ in [one thing]."
"Why won't a bigger company copy you?"Advantage"They could. What we know that matters is [insight], and we're moving by [action]."
"How big is the market?"Reasoning"We count [number of practices] × [yearly price]. Here is where each number comes from."
"What traction do you have?"Progress and speed"Since [month], we've [real progress]."
"How will you get customers?"A believable route"Our first customers come from [channel]. So far [result]."
"How do you make money?"Focus"Practices pay a monthly fee." (State one main model.)
"What could kill this?"Self-awareness"The biggest risk is [risk]. We'll know more after [test]."
"Why you?"Founder insight"I saw this problem when [true experience]."
"What will you do with the money?"Planning"We'll spend it on [two or three things] to reach [milestone]."
"Why has nobody done this?"Depth of research"Some have tried: [examples]. What's different now is [reason]."

On market size, Seibel describes top-down and bottom-up estimates and says he prefers bottom-up. Be ready to show your working.

What to say when you don't know

Do not guess a number. Try: "I don't know yet. What we know is [fact]. We'll find out by [action], and I can send it to you by [day]." Then send it. Following up is a chance to show how you work.

WeakStronger

How to handle pressure and interruptions

  • Interrupted mid-pitch: answer briefly, then say "I'll come back to that on the next point" if you will.
  • Disagreement: ask what they have seen. They may know something useful.
  • Several questions at once: "There are two questions there. On pricing... and on churn..."
  • A blank moment: "Let me think about that." Then pause. (Avoid filling it with ums.)

A 15-minute Q&A drill

  1. Write each question above, plus your own worst ones, on separate cards.
  2. Shuffle them and start recording.
  3. Draw a card, pause, answer within 45 seconds.
  4. On playback, check: did the first sentence answer the question? Where did it drift? Did you claim anything you can't support?
  5. Redo your two weakest answers.

Do this alongside your solo pitch rehearsal, then ask a founder or adviser to question you without warning.

Try this now (five minutes)

  1. Write the one question you most hope nobody asks.
  2. Record your honest answer.
  3. Cut it to an answer sentence plus one fact.
  4. Record it again and compare the length.

Questions people ask

How long should an answer to an investor question be?

Short enough to leave room for a follow-up. Aiming for under 45 seconds in practice is a useful limit; some questions need more.

What if I don't know the answer?

Say you don't know yet, share what you do know, explain how you will find out and follow up when you said you would.

How do I answer "Who are your competitors?"

Name the real alternatives, including how people solve the problem today, then state clearly where you differ.

How do I answer "What could go wrong?"

Name your biggest real risk and the test or step you are using to learn about it. Saying there are no risks is rarely believable.

How can I practise investor Q&A alone?

Put hard questions on cards, draw them at random, record each answer and check that the first sentence answers the question.
