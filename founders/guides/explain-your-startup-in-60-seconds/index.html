How to explain your startup in 60 seconds

By the Founder Rehearsal team · Updated 25 November 2026 · 4 min read

The short answer. Use five parts in order: who it is for, the problem they have, what you do, one piece of real evidence, and what you want from the listener. Use plain words, and keep a two-sentence version ready.

"So what do you do?" is the question you will answer more than any other. A long, tangled answer loses people early. A clear one earns the follow-up question, which is the real goal of a 60-second explanation.

What a 60-second pitch is for

You are not trying to explain everything. In How to Pitch Your Company, Y Combinator's Michael Seibel writes about this kind of question that the aim is to make the listener interested enough to ask more, not to make them understand your whole business. He also advises explaining what you do in the simplest language possible, and removing jargon, marketing speak and ambiguous words such as "platform".

The five-part template

PartFill inAbout
1. Who"We help [specific group] ..."5 seconds
2. Problem"... who currently [what goes wrong, in everyday words]."15 seconds
3. What you do"We [the action your product takes], so they can [result they want]."15 seconds
4. Evidence"So far, [one true fact: customers, usage, revenue, a pilot or interviews]."15 seconds
5. Next step"We're looking for [one clear request]."10 seconds

At a speaking rate of 120 to 150 words a minute, 60 seconds is about 120 to 150 words. Fewer is fine, and leaves room to pause.

A worked example

The company below is made up to show the structure.

"We help small dental practices that lose money when patients don't turn up. Reception staff chase people by phone between other jobs, so many reminders never happen. We send the reminders automatically and fill cancelled slots from a waiting list. We're running our first pilot now, and the practice manager checks it each morning. We're looking for three more practices to test it with."

Notice that there is no "AI-powered end-to-end engagement platform". A listener can picture the receptionist and the empty chair.

What if you have no traction yet?

Do not invent it, and do not stretch it. Say what you have done: "We've interviewed 20 practice managers", "We have a working prototype", or "We start our first pilot next month". Use your own real numbers. An honest early fact is stronger than a vague claim.

Make three lengths

  • One sentence (about 10 seconds): who you help and what you do.
  • Two sentences (about 20 seconds): add the problem. This is your everyday answer.
  • Sixty seconds: all five parts.

Start short. If they ask for more, give more.

Test whether people understand it

Seibel's email test is simple: email your two-sentence explanation to a smart friend and ask them to explain it back in different words. If they have to ask what you mean, revise it. Choose someone outside your industry. Doing it by email stops you filling the gaps as you talk.

Adapt it for the listener

ListenerLead withAsk for
InvestorThe problem and your evidenceA longer meeting
Possible customerThe problem in their wordsA short trial or conversation
Possible hireWhy it matters to youA coffee
Someone at an eventThe one-sentence versionNothing yet: let them ask

Practise it until it sounds like talking

  1. Write it, then read it aloud once. Change anything you would never say in conversation.
  2. Reduce it to five prompt words, one for each part.
  3. Record it from the prompts. Time it.
  4. If it runs long, cut a detail. Don't talk faster. (See speaking slower and more clearly.)
  5. Record it on different days and with different openings, so it doesn't sound recited.

If fillers creep into the opening, our guide to stopping um will help. For the longer version, see how to practise an investor pitch alone.

Try this now (ten minutes)

  1. Fill in all five lines of the template.
  2. Circle every word a 12-year-old would not know, and replace it.
  3. Record it and check it is under 60 seconds.
  4. Email your two-sentence version to one person and ask them to explain it back.

Questions people ask

What should a 60-second startup pitch include?

Who it is for, the problem, what you do, one piece of real evidence and a clear next step.

How many words is a 60-second pitch?

About 120 to 150 words at a steady pace. Fewer words are fine if every part is clear.

Should I start with my name or the problem?

Give your name briefly if the listener doesn't know it, then get to who you help and what you do straight away.

What if my product is very technical?

Describe what changes for the person who uses it. Keep the technical explanation for the follow-up question.

Is an elevator pitch the same as a 60-second pitch?

The terms are often used for the same thing: a short explanation that makes someone want to know more. Keep a shorter version too.
