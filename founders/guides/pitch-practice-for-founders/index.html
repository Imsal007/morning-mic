How to practise an investor pitch alone

By the Founder Rehearsal team · Updated 25 November 2026 · 5 min read

The short answer. Practise out loud, in short sections, and record every run. Fix one thing at a time, then join the sections, time the full pitch, and rehearse the questions separately before you practise in front of people.

Most founders "practise" by reading their slides in their head. It feels like preparation, but your mouth has never said the words. This guide gives you a way to rehearse an investor pitch on your own, so that your first time saying it aloud is not in front of the investor.

Start with what an investor needs to understand

Before you rehearse delivery, check the content. Y Combinator's Michael Seibel sets out the questions he wants answered in How to Pitch Your Company. His main point is that a pitch has to be clear and concise, and that you should explain what you do in the simplest language you can. He advises removing jargon, acronyms, marketing language and vague words.

He also suggests the email test: write a two-sentence explanation of your company, send it to a smart friend and ask them to explain it back in their own words. If they need to ask what you mean, revise it. Do this by email, because in conversation you can't help adding explanations.

Rehearsing an unclear pitch only makes you fluent at being unclear, so run this test first.

Split the pitch into six sections

SectionThe question it answersA useful time limit
OpeningWhat do you do, and for whom?15 seconds
ProblemWhat is going wrong today?30 seconds
ProductWhat do you do about it?45 seconds
EvidenceWhat has actually happened so far?45 seconds
Why youWhat do you know that others don't?20 seconds
AskWhat do you want from this person?15 seconds

These limits add up to about two minutes fifty. They are a starting point: follow the time your meeting or demo day actually gives you.

The solo rehearsal loop

For each section, run the same loop:

  1. Say it once without notes. Let it be messy. You are finding out what you already know.
  2. Listen back. Write down one thing to fix. Only one.
  3. Say it again with that single fix.
  4. Repeat until the point is steady, even if the exact words change from run to run.

When each section holds up on its own, join two, then three, then the whole pitch. If a join feels awkward, write a single bridge sentence, such as "So we built something to fix that."

What to listen for on playback

CheckAsk yourself
ClarityWould someone outside my industry follow this?
First sentenceDo they know what the company does within the first breath?
NumbersDid I say each figure clearly, with what it measures and over what period?
PaceAm I rushing the parts I know best?
FillersWhere do the ums cluster? (How to reduce them.)
The askIs it a direct request?

Seibel makes a related point about the ask: if you want the investor to invest, ask them. "What do you think?" is not an ask.

Pace: how fast should you pitch?

VirtualSpeech measured 32,809 practice presentations in 2026. In its data, the median speaking rate across presentations was 111 words a minute, elevator pitches had a median of 119, and presentations scoring 8 or more out of 10 had a median of 138. The company suggests roughly 120 to 160 words a minute for most presentations. This is data from one training product, not a controlled study, so use it as a rough guide.

To measure yours, count the words in a one-minute run. If you are far above the range, add pauses after your key points. Our guide to speaking slower and more clearly has exercises.

A 7-day rehearsal plan

DayTask
1Record the whole pitch cold. Keep this file: it is your "before". Run the email test.
2Opening and problem, using the solo loop.
3Product and evidence.
4Why you, and the ask. Join all sections once.
5Three full runs against the clock, standing if you will stand.
6Questions only: ten hard ones, answered aloud.
7One full run plus questions, in the real setup (camera, slides, room). Compare with day 1.

If your meeting is tomorrow, do days 1, 2 and 6 in a shorter form. The opening and the questions deserve your time first.

Rehearse the questions separately

A pitch is a set piece. Questions are not, and they need their own practice. Write the ten questions you hope nobody asks. Draw one at random, pause, and answer in under 45 seconds. Our guide to answering hard investor questions gives a structure.

When to stop practising alone

Solo rehearsal builds the words and the habits. It cannot tell you whether a stranger understands you. Once your runs are steady, pitch to one or two people who will be honest: another founder, an adviser, or someone outside your field. Ask them to tell you, in one sentence, what your company does. Their answer is better feedback than "that was great".

Try to avoid using your most wanted investor as your first live audience.

Try this now (ten minutes)

  1. Record your opening and problem in under 45 seconds.
  2. Listen once and write one fix.
  3. Record again.
  4. Send your two-sentence description to one person and ask them to explain it back.

Questions people ask

How many times should I practise my pitch before meeting investors?

There is no proven number. Keep going until each section stays clear without notes, you finish within the time limit, and you can answer your hardest questions briefly.

Should I memorise my investor pitch?

Memorise your first sentence, your key numbers and your ask. For the rest, learn the order of points so you can recover if you are interrupted.

How can I practise a pitch with no audience?

Record yourself on your phone, listen back, fix one thing and record again. Rehearse in short sections first, then join them and time the full run.

How long should an investor pitch be?

Use the time you are given. For a first conversation, prepare a version of about 60 seconds and a fuller one of about three minutes so you can adjust.

How do I stop being nervous before pitching?

Practise aloud until your opening is automatic. One set of experiments also found that telling yourself "I am excited" helped people feel more excited than trying to calm down, though a later replication did not find better performance as judged by observers.
