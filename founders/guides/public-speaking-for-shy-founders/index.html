Public speaking for shy founders: start where nobody can hear you

By the Founder Rehearsal team · Updated 25 November 2026 · 5 min read

The short answer. Build up in small steps. Start by speaking alone with a recorder, move to one trusted person, then a small group, then an audience. Repeat each step until it feels manageable before you take the next.

You can build a good company and still dread being the one who talks about it. If you turn down podcasts, hand the demo to someone else, or rehearse a sentence in your head until the moment passes, this guide is for you. You don't need to become an extrovert. You need a way to speak when it counts.

Shy, introverted or anxious?

These get mixed together, and they are different:

  • Introversion is usually about where you get your energy. Many introverts speak well and then need quiet time afterwards.
  • Shyness is discomfort or hesitation around other people, especially people you don't know.
  • Public speaking anxiety is fear tied to being watched and judged while you speak.

You may recognise one, two or all three. If fear is severe, causes panic, or stops you doing things you need to do, speak to a GP or a qualified therapist. Practice guides like this one are not treatment.

What research says about facing the fear

Gradual practice at the thing you fear, called exposure, is a well-studied approach. A 2022 meta-analysis of 11 studies with 508 participants found that exposure therapy, both in real situations and in virtual reality, produced large reductions in public speaking anxiety compared with control groups. Those were structured psychological interventions, so self-practice should not be expected to match them. The principle is still useful: avoiding speaking gives you no chance to learn that you can cope with it.

Recording yourself is helpful for hearing your delivery, but don't expect it to remove fear on its own. In a controlled study of 77 people with social anxiety, adding videotape feedback did not improve the results of exposure-based treatment. Use recordings to improve specific things, not to judge yourself.

The eight-step speaking ladder

Each step adds a little more exposure. Move up when the current step feels uncomfortable but manageable. Staying on a step for a while is fine.

StepWhat you doMove on when
1Read a short paragraph aloud, alone.You can read it at an easy pace.
2Record yourself answering one easy question about your work.You can play it back without stopping it.
3Record your 60-second company explanation.You can finish without restarting.
4Record on camera, looking at the lens.You can watch it and name one thing that works.
5Say it to one trusted person.You get to the end while they watch.
6Send a short video to a customer or peer.You have sent it without re-recording all evening.
7Speak in a small group: a meet-up introduction or a team update.You can add one point you had not scripted.
8Take a short talk, panel or podcast.You are ready to book another.

Prepare in the way quiet founders often prefer

  • Write first, then speak. Draft your points, cut them to three, and rehearse from three short prompts.
  • Learn your first two sentences. The start is often the hardest part.
  • Bring a real story. A specific customer moment (shared with permission) is easier to tell than abstract claims.
  • Plan your pauses. Quiet can read as calm. You don't need to fill it.
  • Choose formats that suit you first. A one-to-one podcast or a small round table may feel easier than a stage.

What to do with nerves on the day

You may not be able to switch nerves off, and trying hard to calm down can feel like one more task. In experiments by Alison Wood Brooks, people who reframed anxiety as excitement, for example by saying "I am excited", felt more excited and performed better than those told to calm down. A 2025 replication of the public speaking experiment found similar changes in how speakers felt, but observers did not rate their performance higher. So it is a cheap thing to try for how you feel, not a guarantee of a better talk.

Other practical steps:

  • Arrive early and say your first line in the room, if you can.
  • Take one slow breath before you begin.
  • Put your attention on one friendly or neutral face.
  • If you lose your place, pause, look at your three prompts and carry on. You don't need to apologise.

The "3-2-1" idea, made practical

People searching for speaking help often come across "3-2-1" rules, and different coaches use the name for different things. One simple version you can use for unplanned answers is: three seconds before you speak, two supporting points at most, and one main message to end on. It is a memory aid, not a research finding.

How to measure progress without being hard on yourself

After each practice, write two lines: one thing that went better and one thing to try next. Every two weeks, record the same 60-second answer and compare it with your first. Count what you did, such as steps taken and talks given, as well as how you sounded. Doing it while nervous counts.

Try this now (five minutes)

  1. Alone, record one minute answering: "What made you start this company?"
  2. Listen once. Write down one sentence that sounded like you.
  3. Record again, starting with that sentence.
  4. Choose the step on the ladder you will try this week.

Questions people ask

Can a shy person become a good public speaker?

Yes, many people improve with preparation and gradual practice. The goal is to communicate clearly, not to change your personality.

How do introverts handle public speaking?

Many do well by preparing in writing, practising aloud in private, using a few clear points and planning quiet time afterwards.

Why am I fine one to one but nervous in groups?

A group can feel like being watched and judged. Practise in steps: one person, then two or three, then a small group, before a larger audience.

What is the root cause of shyness?

There is no single cause. Temperament and experience can both play a part. If it limits your life, a qualified professional can help you understand it.

Should I record myself if I hate hearing my voice?

Start with audio, listen once and look for one specific thing to improve. If recording makes you very distressed, begin with an earlier step such as reading aloud.

When should I get professional help for fear of public speaking?

If you have panic attacks, avoid important parts of work or life, or the fear is getting worse, talk to a GP or a qualified therapist.
