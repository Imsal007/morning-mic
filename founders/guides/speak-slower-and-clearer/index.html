How to speak slower and more clearly

By the Founder Rehearsal team · Updated 25 November 2026 · 5 min read

The short answer. Measure your words per minute, then add pauses at the end of ideas rather than stretching every word. Open your mouth a little more, finish word endings and stress the one word in each sentence that carries your meaning.

If people ask you to repeat yourself, or you finish a five-minute update in three, clarity is worth practising. Speaking clearly is not about changing your accent. It is about giving the listener enough time and enough sound to follow you.

How fast should you speak?

There is no single correct speed. VirtualSpeech's 2026 data from 32,809 practice presentations shows these medians:

Situation or groupMedian words per minute
All presentations111
Meeting room presentations105
Conference and event talks116
Elevator pitches119
Presentations scoring 8 or more out of 10138

The company suggests roughly 120 to 160 words a minute for most presentations. It also notes that faster speakers scored higher up to about 180, and that a very slow rate often reflects hesitation. So slowing down is not automatically better. The aim is a pace your listener can follow, with real pauses. This is one training company's data, and rates are not directly comparable between languages.

Measure your pace in two minutes

  1. Record yourself explaining your work for exactly one minute, without reading.
  2. Use your phone's transcription, or type out what you said.
  3. Count the words. That is your words per minute.
  4. Listen again and mark where you ran sentences together.

Why you speed up

  • You are nervous and want to finish.
  • You know the material so well that you forget the listener is hearing it for the first time.
  • You have too much content for the time.
  • Silence feels uncomfortable, so you fill it.

The third one matters most before a pitch. If you have to rush to fit, cut content. See the 60-second template.

Nine exercises for slower, clearer speech

1. Pause at every full stop

Read a paragraph aloud and count "one" silently at each full stop. It will feel exaggerated. Play it back and judge how it sounds.

2. Mark your script with slashes

Write one slash where you will take a short pause and two where you will take a longer one: "We help dental practices // that lose money / when patients don't turn up."

3. Breathe before the important sentence

Take an easy breath before your key number or your ask. It creates a natural pause and supports your voice.

4. Finish your word endings

Practise sentences with final consonants: "We built it, tested it and shipped it last month." Make the last sound of each word audible without over-pronouncing it.

5. Open your mouth more

Mumbling often comes with very little jaw movement. Read a passage with slightly larger movements, then relax to a comfortable level and record both.

6. Slow down your hard words

List the product names, technical terms and numbers you stumble on. Say each slowly by syllable, then inside a full sentence.

7. Stress one word per sentence

Underline the word that carries the meaning. Compare "We fill empty slots" with "We fill empty slots". Stress shows the listener what to notice.

8. Let your voice fall at the end of a statement

If every sentence rises at the end, statements can sound like questions. Record "We launch in March" and listen to the final word.

9. Shadow a clear speaker

Play a short clip of a speaker you find easy to follow. Repeat each phrase just after them, copying their pauses and stress. You are borrowing rhythm, not an accent.

Speaking English as a second language

You don't need to sound like a native speaker to be clear. Concentrate on the things that often affect understanding:

  • Word stress: check the stressed syllable of your key words in a dictionary with audio.
  • Endings: plural and past-tense endings can change meaning.
  • Chunking: speak in short groups of words with small pauses between them.
  • Simple sentences: one idea each is easier to say and to follow.

Ask a listener which words they missed. Practise those first.

If you have a sudden or worsening change in your speech, or pain or hoarseness that does not clear, see a GP. A speech and language therapist can help with speech disorders; this guide covers everyday presentation practice.

Try this now (five minutes)

  1. Record one minute about your product and count the words.
  2. Write your three key sentences with pause slashes and one stressed word each.
  3. Record the minute again.
  4. Compare word count and clarity. If you can, ask someone which version was easier to follow.

Questions people ask

How do I stop mumbling and speak clearly?

Move your jaw and lips a little more, finish word endings, take a breath before important sentences, and record yourself to find the words that blur.

How can I reduce my speaking speed?

Add pauses at the end of ideas, mark them in your notes, and cut content that forces you to rush. Stretching each word usually sounds unnatural.

What is a good speaking pace for a presentation?

One company's data on 32,809 practice presentations suggests roughly 120 to 160 words a minute for most presentations. Adjust for your audience, language and material.

Does speaking slowly make you sound more confident?

Not by itself. Deliberate pauses can sound composed, but very slow, hesitant speech can sound unsure.

Do I need to change my accent to be understood?

No. Pace, word stress, clear endings and simple sentences are usually more useful to work on than your accent.
